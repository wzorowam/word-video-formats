## Visual system and animation

One object, center frame: a matte, unbranded bottle. No room, no street, no pointing hands, no dots. Color shifts carry the beats: charcoal, cold blue, warm gray, paper, amber, liquid pale-green. The peel is a paper card with a soft shadow. The force is a clipped gradient plus a slow ripple, not a character.

**agent** is set in a heavy grotesque, one glyph per letter. Letters translate on x, overshoot, and settle, or ride the peel and the liquid. Nothing else is kinetic type. Captions are a small static sans, opacity only, two words at most, so a muted viewer can follow and a hearing viewer is not reading the narration twice.

Sound: a warm conversational voice, about 140 words a minute, slower on each spoken *agent*. Music under the voice. One soft tick only when the visual state changes. Silence from 22.00 to 24.50.

## What the episode teaches

**Myth:** An agent is a person you picture with a job — a spy, a realtor, someone you pay to act for you.

**Fact:** In the earliest English evidence, *agent* is a force that acts on matter. Thomas Norton’s *Ordinal of Alchemy* names heat, cold, moisture, or dryness as the principal agent. The hire comes later: “deputy, representative” is attested from the 1590s.

The bleach line is the same fact in a cupboard, not a second definition. The liquid acts. The name on the bottle does not.

## Series plan

Named, numbered series: **Under the Label #1–#5.** Same bottle, same peel, same retrieval chips. Later episodes spend about two seconds on a chip quiz of one or two earlier words before their own pause, then mark the answer and move on.

1. **agent** (this episode). Myth: a person you hire. Fact: earliest English use is a force that acts on matter; heat can be the agent.
2. **nice.** Myth: nice has always meant pleasant. Fact: in Middle English it meant foolish, from Latin *nescius*, “not knowing.” Quiz #1: agent — the person you pay, or the force that acts?
3. **silly.** Myth: silly has always meant foolish. Fact: Old English *gesælig* meant happy, fortunate, blessed; the foolish sense is much later (16th century). Quiz #1 and #2: agent (force) and nice (once foolish).
4. **meat.** Myth: meat has always meant animal flesh. Fact: Old English *mete* meant food in general; fifteenth-century “green meat” could be vegetables. Quiz #2 and #3: nice (once foolish) and silly (once happy).
5. **girl.** Myth: girl has always meant a female child. Fact: about 1300, *gyrle* meant a young person of either sex, though already most often female; the specific “female child” sense is later. Quiz #1 and #4: agent (the force) and meat (once any food).

## Fact check

The spoken claim is narrow on purpose. Medieval Latin *agent-* could already mean a person authorized to act, and Etymonline glosses late-15th-century English *agent* as “one who acts,” then dates “any natural force or substance which produces a phenomenon” to the 1550s. What the episode asserts, and what the earliest English dictionary evidence supports, is the other half: the hire you picture is not the early sense.

- Merriam-Webster: Middle English *agent*, “force capable of acting on matter”; first known use in the 15th century in that sense. https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/agent
- Middle English Dictionary: “a force capable of acting upon matter, an active principle.” Norton’s *Ordinal of Alchemy*: the principal agent is sometimes heat, sometimes cold, sometimes moisture, sometimes dryness. https://quod.lib.umich.edu/m/middle-english-dictionary/dictionary/MED830
- Oxford English Dictionary: earliest evidence before 1500, George Ripley. https://www.oed.com/dictionary/agent_n1
- Etymonline: “deputy, representative” from the 1590s. That is the job in the myth. Secret-agent is later still (1916) and is not taught here. https://www.etymonline.com/word/agent

Series facts, for the one-line premises only:

- nice: Middle English “foolish, ignorant,” from Latin *nescius* “not knowing.” https://www.etymonline.com/word/nice
- silly: Old English *gesælig* “happy, fortuitous, prosperous”; foolish sense from the 1570s. https://www.etymonline.com/word/silly
- meat: Old English *mete* “food, nourishment”; flesh sense narrows later; *grene-mete* for vegetables in the 15th century. https://www.etymonline.com/word/meat
- girl: c. 1300 *gyrle* “child, young person” of either sex, most frequently female; specific “female child” late 14th century. https://www.etymonline.com/word/girl
